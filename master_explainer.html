The Problem We're Solving

The current job market is broken. Here's why:

🎯 Zero-Cost Applications

Candidates can apply to unlimited jobs at no cost. This leads to "spray and pray" behavior where people apply to everything, overwhelming employers with unqualified applications.

🤖 AI Has Killed Traditional Signals

Cover letters and application effort used to signal genuine interest. Now AI writes them in seconds. Employers can no longer distinguish between genuine and mass applications.

📉 Adverse Selection

The best candidates often don't apply—they're already employed or only looking at specific opportunities. The worst candidates apply everywhere. This creates a "lemons problem" for employers.

📝 Vague Job Descriptions

Employers write intentionally vague descriptions to "maximize optionality." This means candidates can't self-select, leading to even more mismatched applications.

👻 No Accountability

Ghosting is endemic on both sides. Phantom job listings waste candidate time. There's no cost to bad behavior.

The Core Insight: When something costs nothing, people treat it as worthless. By adding meaningful stakes on both sides, we create real signals and accountability.

Why These Numbers?

Candidate Fees: Small but Meaningful

At 0.025% of salary, fees range from $12.50 for a $50K role to $125 for a $500K role. This is enough to make people think twice about applying randomly, but not so high as to exclude qualified candidates.

Employer Stakes: Skin in the Game

At 2.5% of salary, employers stake $1,250–$12,500 depending on the role. This is significant enough to incentivize clear job descriptions and efficient processes, but far less than traditional recruiting fees (typically 15-25% of salary).

Candidate Outcomes

When you apply for a job, there are three possible outcomes. Each has different financial implications.

The Bonus Pool: All fees from failed candidates are pooled together and split among candidates who accept offers. In a typical scenario with 40 applicants, 2 acceptances, and $50 fees: each accepted candidate gets $50 (refund) + $900 (share of $1,800 pool) = $950.

The Incentive Structure

This creates a powerful self-selection mechanism:

  • Well-calibrated candidates (who apply only where they're likely to succeed) have positive expected value
  • Poorly-calibrated candidates (who spray applications) will lose money over time
  • Declining an offer proves you can get offers but doesn't profit—encouraging honest job-seeking

The Scoring Problem

Simple median comparison sounds fair, but it breaks down in practice. Here's why we need something smarter.

Problem 1: Time Windows Are Arbitrary

If we compare employers "in the same month," what happens to someone who hired on March 31st vs April 1st? They're in different cohorts despite being one day apart.

A hire on day 1 of a month vs day 30 creates unfair comparisons with employers 29 days apart while ignoring employers 2 days apart in different months.

Problem 2: Salary Bands Are Crude

If we compare "$100K-$150K roles," what about a $149K role vs a $151K role? They're nearly identical but in different cohorts.

These two roles are $2K apart but compared to completely different cohorts.

The Solution: Continuous Comparison

Instead of discrete buckets, we use kernel functions to create smooth, continuous comparisons. Every employer is compared to every other employer, but with weights that reflect how similar they are in terms of timing and salary.

Key Insight: An employer hiring at $150K should be compared strongly to one at $145K, somewhat to one at $120K, and barely to one at $50K. Kernel functions make this mathematically precise.

Kernel Functions

A kernel function measures similarity between two things. It outputs a number between 0 (completely different) and 1 (identical).

The Gaussian Kernel

We use the Gaussian (bell curve) kernel. It's smooth, intuitive, and well-understood mathematically.

K(x) = exp(-x² / 2σ²)
σ (width parameter) 1.0

When σ is small, only very similar items count. When σ is large, even distant items have some influence.

Why Gaussian? The bell curve naturally captures the intuition that "very similar = high weight, somewhat similar = medium weight, very different = low weight" with smooth transitions.

Time Kernel (Recency)

Recent hires are more relevant than old ones. Market conditions change, so a hire from 6 months ago is less informative than one from last week.

K_time(Δt) = exp(-Δt / τ)
where Δt = days between hires, τ = decay rate (default: 30 days)

Salary Kernel (Role Similarity)

Similar salaries indicate similar roles. But we use log-salary because percentage differences matter more than absolute differences.

Why Log-Salary?

A $10K difference means different things at different levels:

  • $50K vs $60K = 20% increase (significant!)
  • $200K vs $210K = 5% increase (minor)

Using log-salary makes these comparisons proportional.

K_salary(s₁, s₂) = exp(-(log(s₁) - log(s₂))² / 2σ²)
where σ controls how strict the salary matching is (default: 0.5)

The final weight for comparing two employers combines both kernels:

W = K_time × K_salary

This means an employer must be similar in both time and salary to have high influence. Being recent but different salary? Low weight. Same salary but old? Low weight.

Normalization: Expected Efficiency

Different salary levels have different "normal" efficiency ratios. Entry-level roles attract more applicants than executive roles. We need to account for this.

The Problem

A ratio of 30 might be excellent for a $50K role (which attracts many applicants) but poor for a $300K role (which attracts few). Comparing raw ratios across salary levels isn't fair.

The Solution: E(s)

We calculate the expected efficiency E(s) at each salary level using weighted averages:

E(s) = Σ(W_j × ratio_j) / Σ(W_j)
where W_j is the combined weight for employer j

Lower salaries typically have higher expected ratios (more applicants per hire).

Personalized Benchmark

Your benchmark isn't a single number—it's calculated specifically for your salary level using weighted contributions from all other employers:

Your Benchmark = E(your salary) = weighted average of similar employers' ratios
Fair Comparison: You're compared against what's expected for employers at your salary level, weighted by how similar they are to you in both time and salary.

Effective Sample Size (ESS)

What if there aren't many similar employers to compare against? We need to handle sparse data gracefully.

The Problem

If you're hiring at an unusual salary (say $275K) or in a new market, there might be only 2-3 employers with meaningful weights. Basing your benchmark on such thin data is risky.

The Solution: ESS

Effective Sample Size measures how much "real" data we have after weighting:

ESS = (Σ W_j)² / Σ(W_j²)

Shrinkage: Blending Local and Global

When ESS is low, we blend the local benchmark with a global average:

α = ESS / (ESS + k) where k ≈ 10

Why Shrinkage? It's better to be slightly biased toward the global average than to have wild swings based on 2-3 data points. As more data accumulates, shrinkage automatically decreases.

Zero-Sum Batch Normalization

The employer scoring system should be zero-sum: money lost by below-benchmark employers should fund bonuses for above-benchmark employers.

The Problem

If we calculate multipliers independently for each employer, there's no guarantee they balance out. We might pay out more than we collect, or vice versa.

The Solution: Batch Normalization

We process employers in batches and adjust multipliers to ensure zero-sum:

Adjustment Factor = Total Penalties / Total Bonuses

Adjusted Bonus = Original Bonus × Adjustment Factor
The Result: Employers compete against each other in a closed system. Better employers profit at the expense of worse employers, with no net flow in or out of the system.

The Complete Algorithm

Here's how everything fits together when processing a batch of employers.

Step-by-Step Details

Step 1: Calculate Weights

For each pair of employers (i, j), compute:

W_ij = exp(-Δt/τ) × exp(-(log(s_i) - log(s_j))² / 2σ²)
Step 2: Compute Expected Efficiency

For employer i's benchmark:

E(s_i) = Σ(W_ij × ratio_j) / Σ(W_ij)
Step 3: Calculate ESS

Measure data quality for employer i:

ESS_i = (Σ W_ij)² / Σ(W_ij²)
Step 4: Apply Shrinkage

Blend local and global benchmarks:

α = ESS / (ESS + 10)
Benchmark_i = α × E(s_i) + (1-α) × Global_Avg
Step 5: Compute Multipliers

How employer i compares to their benchmark:

Multiplier_i = clamp(Benchmark_i / ratio_i, 0.5, 1.5)
Step 6: Zero-Sum Normalize

Adjust all multipliers so total payouts balance:

Adjustment = Σ(penalties) / Σ(bonuses)
Final_Multiplier = adjusted to balance

Summary

The platform creates a bilateral calibration market where:

  1. Candidates stake money on their self-assessed fit
  2. Employers stake money on their description clarity
  3. Well-calibrated candidates profit from poorly-calibrated ones
  4. Efficient employers profit from inefficient ones
  5. The platform takes a cut of successful matches
  6. Market prices emerge through iteration, not guesswork

The mechanism requires no central authority to judge "quality." It only requires that miscalibration costs money and accuracy is rewarded. The iterated game does the rest.

The Problem with Discrete Comparison

Any discrete boundary creates an edge to exploit. We need smooth, continuous comparison instead.

Fixed Time Windows

Employers rush to close searches at quarter-end to game the comparison set.

March 31 competes against different employers than April 1

Salary Bands

A $149k role vs $151k role land in different "leagues" despite being nearly identical.

Creates incentive to manipulate salary to land in favorable band

What We Want: Continuous Comparison

  • Smooth, continuous comparison with no cliffs or edges
  • Similar employers (in time and salary) compared more strongly
  • Everyone contributes something to everyone's comparison
  • Total money out = total money in (zero-sum)

What is a Kernel Function?

A kernel measures similarity between two things. It outputs a number between 0 and 1:

1.0
Very similar
Strong comparison
0.5
Moderate similarity
Medium comparison
0.0
Very different
Weak/no comparison

Think of it as asking: "How much should employer A's performance affect how we judge employer B?"

Gaussian Kernel: K(x, y) = exp(-(x - y)2 / 2σ2)
1.0

Discrete Bands (The Problem)

You're either "in" (weight = 1) or "out" (weight = 0)

$140k vs $145k: weight = 1
$140k vs $155k: weight = 0 ← cliff!

Kernel Approach (The Solution)

Smooth transition, no cliffs to exploit

$140k vs $145k: weight = 0.97
$140k vs $160k: weight = 0.73
$140k vs $200k: weight = 0.32

Time Kernel: When Did Searches Close?

Employers who closed their searches at similar times should be compared more strongly.

Ktime(i, j) = exp(-|ti - tj| / τ)
ti, tj = close times | τ (tau) = decay constant (e.g., 30 days)
Time Decay Visualization
30
Time Difference Kernel Value (τ=30) Interpretation

Why Exponential Decay?

The job market changes over time. Comparing your January search to someone's June search is less meaningful than comparing two January searches. Exponential decay naturally captures "recency matters."

Salary Kernel: Similar Role Values

Employers hiring for similar salary levels should be compared more strongly.

Ksalary(i, j) = exp(-(log(si) - log(sj))2 / σ2)
si, sj = salaries | σ = proximity scale (e.g., 0.5 in log space)

Why Log-Salary?

Relative differences matter more than absolute ones:

$100k → $110k = 10% jump
$200k → $210k = 5% jump

Both are $10k apart, but the first is "bigger" in relative terms.

Log Difference Comparison

log($110k) - log($100k) = 0.095
log($210k) - log($200k) = 0.049

Log correctly shows the first gap is "bigger".

Combined Weights: Time × Salary

Two employers are strongly compared only if they're similar in both dimensions.

Wij = Ktime(i, j) × Ksalary(i, j)
Combined weight = time similarity × salary similarity
Weight Heatmap: Employer A ($150k, Jan 15) vs Others

Key Insight

Employer A ($150k, Jan 15) is compared most strongly to employers with similar salaries who closed around the same time. Employers far away in either dimension have low weights.

Normalizing Across Salary Levels

Raw efficiency varies systematically with salary. We need to normalize for fair comparison.

The Problem

Raw efficiency (accepts/applicants) varies by salary:

Entry-level: 0.01-0.03 (many applicants)
Senior: 0.05-0.10
Executive: 0.10-0.20 (few applicants)

Every executive would "beat" every entry-level!

The Solution: E(s)

Learn what's "normal" at each salary level:

n = actual_efficiency / E(s)
Normalized = actual / expected for that salary
Expected Efficiency E(s) by Salary

A $60k 100 2 0.020 0.025 0.80
B $150k 40 2 0.050 0.040 1.25
C $250k 20 2 0.100 0.080 1.25

Raw efficiency suggests C is 5x better than A. After normalization, B and C are equal (both 25% above their salary's average).

Personalized Benchmarks

Each employer gets a personalized benchmark—a weighted average of similar employers' performances.

benchmarki = Σj(Wij × nj) / Σj(Wij)
Your benchmark = weighted average of others' normalized efficiency, where similar employers count more
How Weights Affect Your Benchmark
ri = ni / benchmarki
r > 1.0: Beat benchmark | r = 1.0: Matched | r < 1.0: Underperformed

Contextual Comparison

If most similar employers did well, your benchmark is high—you need to do well to beat it. If they did poorly, your benchmark is low—easier to beat. You're judged against your relevant peers.

Handling Sparse Data: ESS

What if you're an early employer or posting a unique salary? We need to measure comparison confidence.

ESSi = (Σj Wij)2 / Σj(Wij)2

Zero-Sum Normalization

Total money distributed must equal total money staked. We achieve this through batch normalization.

Calculate Raw Returns
For each employer: RawReturn = Stake × adjusted_r
Sum and Compare
Total Stakes vs Total Raw Returns (usually don't match)
Calculate Scale Factor
k = Total Stakes / Total Raw Returns
Apply Scaling
FinalReturn = RawReturn × k (now sums exactly to stakes)
Batch Normalization Example

Why Batching?

Money needs to actually transfer. Weekly batching gives us predictable payouts, clean accounting, and manageable sizes—while still being much smoother than quarterly windows.

The Core Principle

Replace discrete boundaries with smooth, continuous functions. Instead of asking "which box are you in?", ask "how similar are you to everyone else?"
